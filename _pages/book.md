My book manuscript, ***Domesticating the International: The Uneven Enforcement of Investors' Preferences and Its Unintended Consequences***, explains how a domestic American court became a governing institution of international sovereign finance—and why the enforcement regime it enabled nearly disappeared soon after reaching its greatest power.

For much of US history, the doctrine of absolute sovereign immunity insulated foreign governments from suit in American courts. Creditors had little legal recourse when governments defaulted because a sovereign could not be brought before a foreign judge without its consent. The 1952 Tate Letter began to dismantle this arrangement by announcing that the United States would follow the restrictive theory of sovereign immunity: governmental acts would remain protected, but commercial conduct could be adjudicated. The 1976 Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) subsequently codified that distinction and transferred responsibility for applying it from the State Department to federal courts.

This transition did not simply replace diplomacy with neutral legal rules. It relocated political judgment from the executive branch to the judiciary and made the boundary between sovereign authority and commercial activity a question for American judges. The result is a condition I call *juridical sovereignty*: foreign states retain formal independence, but their practical autonomy over borrowing, default, and restructuring is constrained by the interpretive choices of domestic courts.

The book's central argument is that US federal courts—and the Southern District of New York (SDNY) above all—became the *de facto* regulators of international sovereign debt markets. This outcome was not designed by Congress, the executive branch, or an international organization. A State Department memorandum, a domestic statute, New York-law contracts, financial infrastructure, and the ordinary machinery of private litigation combined to create an enforcement regime with global reach. Because foreign sovereign bonds are frequently issued under New York law and serviced through New York-linked financial institutions, decisions made in a federal courthouse in lower Manhattan acquired consequences far beyond the United States.

Yet the legal machinery was not self-executing. Sovereigns entered the courtroom because their debts had been contractually domesticated; specialist investors selected and financed claims; law firms accumulated the professional knowledge required to turn judgments into leverage; and judges determined which enforcement strategies the law would permit. Court decisions then generated authoritative information about what creditors could credibly attempt—disrupting payment channels, locating assets, pursuing attachment, or pressing claims in other jurisdictions. Markets and firms responded to those changes in the legal opportunity set, not simply to the identity of the party that formally won.

The regime also generated its own correction. After litigation against Argentina demonstrated the reach of the holdout strategy, sovereign issuers and market participants rewrote the contractual provisions on which that strategy depended. New lawsuits subsequently collapsed even as the stock of defaulted sovereign debt rose. The same decentralized institutional process that created the court-centered regime therefore also curtailed it.

The manuscript draws on three original datasets: sovereign-immunity requests submitted to the State Department from 1952 to 1977; commercial litigation involving foreign sovereigns in US courts from 1811 to 1977; and sovereign-debt litigation in the SDNY from 1977 through March 2022. I combine these records with data on the broader universe of sovereign defaults and restructurings, judicial appointments, sovereign bond prices and spreads, international trade, and debt contracts. ## Origins and Activation

**1. Sovereignty over Commerce: The Tate Letter and the Unwritten Bond Exemption, 1952–1976.** This chapter traces the United States' transition from absolute to restrictive sovereign immunity. Although the Tate Letter announced that foreign states would no longer receive immunity for their commercial conduct, it provided no clear criteria for distinguishing governmental from commercial acts. The State Department continued to make decisions in response to foreign-policy pressures, while courts applied the new policy inconsistently. Most importantly, sovereign borrowing and default remained largely protected. Both institutions treated public debt as an internal act of fiscal governance, producing an unwritten bond exemption that persisted until the FSIA.

**2. Who Gets Sued: Selection into the American Courtroom.** Litigation is not an automatic consequence of sovereign default. Comparing SDNY cases with the full universe of sovereign default and restructuring episodes since the FSIA, this chapter identifies the legal architecture that exposes some sovereigns—but not others—to American judicial authority. New York-law obligations, dollar-denominated instruments, access to New York-linked financial infrastructure, and restructurings that leave creditor claims alive make litigation possible. The relevant form of exposure also changes with the market: arrears and syndicated loans structured entry during the first litigation wave, while dollar bonds governed the second. Creditors selected sovereigns already experiencing economic deterioration rather than those poised for recovery, an important source of selection that the book carries into its later analyses.

**3. The Operators.** The FSIA created the legal capacity to sue sovereign states, but legal rights do not enforce themselves. This chapter explains how creditors and their lawyers activate the institutional machinery of sovereign-debt enforcement. Distressed-debt investors identify and acquire claims for which litigation is part of the expected return, while specialist law firms accumulate the procedural, doctrinal, and cross-border expertise required to convert judgments into leverage. Because this capability develops through repeated cases and durable fund–counsel relationships, it remains concentrated even when the underlying legal doctrines are publicly available. The chapter therefore distinguishes legal capacity from operating capacity and shows that the reach of a legal regime depends not only on statutes and judicial decisions, but also on the organizations able and willing to use them.

## Operation and Consequences

**4. [Democracy, Debt, and the Bench: Political Ideology in Sovereign Immunity Cases](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020818326101398).** *(Published online in* International Organization, *First View, 2026.)* Although the FSIA established a framework favorable to contract enforcement, judicial outcomes remain variable. Republican-nominated judges consistently apply the statute's pro-creditor framework across defendant types. Democratic-nominated judges exhibit conditional deference: they are more willing to defer to a defaulting democracy that demonstrated fiscal restraint beforehand, but enforce more strictly when high government consumption suggests that default reflected strategic choice rather than necessity. For autocratic defendants, whose fiscal behavior provides a less credible signal of genuine distress, judges nominated by both parties converge on pro-creditor rulings. The analysis draws on an original dataset of sovereign-debt litigation from 1977 to 2022.

**5. Information Clearinghouse: Market Implications of US Judicial Decisions.** US courts shape international financial markets not by seizing sovereign treasuries or compelling repayment directly, but by changing the legal opportunity set available to private creditors. The chapter classifies rulings according to the enforcement channel they open. Decisions that enable creditors to disrupt ordinary debt service produce immediate and persistent spread widening. Decisions that expand asset discovery can increase the expected recovery value of eligible claims. Confirmatory rulings and decisions that provide no new enforcement tool produce little persistent market response. The direction of repricing therefore follows the legal substance of the ruling—not simply whether the creditor or sovereign formally prevailed.

**6. Courts, Contracts, and Supply Chains: Judicial Signals and the Trade Consequences of Sovereign Default.** This chapter extends the informational argument from financial markets into the real economy. Creditor-favorable rulings are followed by increased intermediate-goods imports in countries experiencing severe defaults, while defendant-favorable rulings produce no comparable response. The effect is substantially larger for capital goods than for consumer goods, consistent with the argument that judicial signals matter most for transactions requiring stable, relationship-specific contractual investments. Courts therefore affect not only sovereign borrowing costs but also the willingness of firms to form and restore international supply relationships.

## Aftermath

**7. After the Gavel: The Fading of the Holdout Era.** Mass sovereign-debt litigation was not the ordinary response to the ordinary default. It was an Argentina-centered enforcement industry sustained by an exceptional stock of vulnerable bonds, repeat creditors, specialist counsel, and usable financial pressure points. The Argentina litigation ultimately demonstrated that a small group of creditors could use New York-law contracts and payment infrastructure to disrupt service on restructured debt. But that success revealed the vulnerabilities of the existing contractual architecture. Enhanced collective-action clauses and revised *pari passu* language reduced the future supply of bonds susceptible to the strategy, while Argentina's 2016 settlements removed the inventory around which the industry had formed. New SDNY filings subsequently fell by an order of magnitude relative to the stock of defaulted sovereign bonds.

The disappearance of mass holdout litigation does not mean that creditor power disappeared. Instead, enforcement migrated from the docket into bond contracts and collective voting rules, while a growing share of sovereign-debt conflict moved toward official-creditor negotiations and domestic legislative debates in New York. The regime's rise and partial recession reveal creditor power to be institutionally constructed, historically contingent, and capable of provoking adaptations that transform the foundations on which it rests.
